# Early Earth and the road life took

Research for Primordia, gathered 2026-10-11, and what it means for the engine. Dates in Ga are billions of years ago, Ma millions. "PAL" is the present level of oxygen in the air. Dates from molecular clocks depend on the model used; points the sources disagree on are marked contested.

## Part 1. How life arose and diversified

### Where it began (no consensus)

- **Alkaline vents.** Hydrogen-rich alkaline fluid meets a carbon-dioxide-rich, more acid sea across thin iron-sulfide walls. That gives a proton gradient of the size and direction living cells still use. It needs a sea with no oxygen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and metal sulfides. How the carbon was first reduced is unknown. ([Sojo 2016](https://marineinfo.org/doc/publication/329236))
- **RNA first.** Well supported as a stage, but nobody has shown the first nucleotides or a self-copying RNA forming unaided. ([Martin 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4390851))
- **Warm ponds and hot springs.** Wetting and drying joins small molecules into long ones. It needs exposed land, fresh water and a supply of organics. ([Pearce 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5664559), [Damer & Deamer 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7133448))

### The first cell we can reconstruct

- The last common ancestor of everything alive lived about 4.2 Ga, with around 2,600 proteins. It needed no oxygen, built itself from hydrogen and carbon dioxide (making acetate), had an early immune system, and so already lived among other things. ([Moody 2024](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10195667/))
- An earlier reconstruction adds: fixed its own nitrogen, lived hot. Whether it lived hot is contested. ([Weiss 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/nmicrobiol.2016.116), [Catchpole & Forterre 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6878951/))

### Ways of making a living, in the order they appeared

| When | Way of living | Takes in | Gives off | Pays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ~4.2 Ga | Acetate-making | Hydrogen, carbon dioxide | Acetate | About 0.3 ATP per acetate |
| by ~3.5 Ga | Methane-making | Hydrogen, carbon dioxide | Methane | Under 0.5 ATP per methane |
| early | Fermenting what others leave | Dead matter | Acids, alcohol | 2 ATP per sugar |

### Oxygen was a poison first

- Between 2.46 and 2.06 Ga oxygen in the air rose from about 0.001% to as much as 10% of today's. ([Great Oxidation Event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Oxidation_Event))
- Oxygen breaks the metal centres of the very enzymes early life ran on: those that handle hydrogen, fix nitrogen and make methane. ([Lu & Imlay 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9191689))
- Defences had to be made: enzymes that disarm oxygen's reactive forms. Life that never made them survives where oxygen does not reach: sediment, vents, guts.
- A nitrogen-fixer that also makes oxygen needs a special cell. In filaments about one cell in ten gives up harvesting light, thickens its wall and fixes nitrogen for its neighbours, who feed it. ([Heterocyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterocyst))

### Mats: the first ecosystems

- Layered mats a few millimetres thick are the oldest widely accepted fossils, 3.43–3.48 Ga. ([Allwood 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41586-018-0610-4))
- Each layer lives on its neighbour's waste. On top, oxygen-makers in the light. Below them, light-users that need sulfide rising from beneath. At the dark base, fermenters, methane-makers and sulfate-breathers. ([Ellsworth 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12779409/))

### Genes passed sideways

- Between 1.6% and 33% of a microbe's genes arrived recently from another lineage, and over time about four fifths of genes have moved this way. ([Boto 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2842723))

### Complex cells

- A complex cell is a merger: an archaeon took in a bacterium that became the mitochondrion, some time between 2.05 and 1.2 Ga. Fossils of complex cells date from about 1.65 Ga. ([Tobiasson 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12338589/), [Betts 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6152910))
- Why it mattered: thousands of internal power stations give a cell vastly more energy for each gene it runs, enough to be large and complicated (contested in detail). A cell that swallows prey without them would have to eat about 34 times its weight for each division. ([Martin 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5584316))
- Sex was already present in the ancestor of all complex cells. ([Malik 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2488364))
- Algae began the same way: a complex cell swallowed a light-harvesting bacterium and kept it, about 1.9 Ga. ([Sánchez-Baracaldo 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5604047))

### Bodies, animals and land

- Many-celled life arose at least 25 separate times. Oldest fossil 1.63 Ga. ([Miao 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10807817/))
- In the laboratory, a single-celled alga exposed to a predator that swallows small prey formed eight-cell colonies too big to swallow in under 100 generations. ([Herron 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41598-019-39558-8))
- The first large animals (571–541 Ma) were mostly fixed in place and absorbed their food. From 538.8 Ma, within 13–25 million years, came predators, eyes, shells and burrowing. Flesh-eaters are scarce wherever oxygen is low. ([Sperling 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3746845))
- Land was taken in order: microbial crusts (3.22 Ga), then fungi and plants (about 470–425 Ma), then arthropods, then four-legged animals (about 390–375 Ma). Plants needed a waxy skin, pores, tough spores and stiffened vessels. An ozone layer like today's forms once oxygen reaches about 1% of the present level. ([Homann 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41561-018-0190-9), [ozone models](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.10557))

### Numbers worth keeping

- A bacterium is about 1 cubic micrometre; a yeast cell about 42. ([BioNumbers](https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=106307))
- A microbe makes about 0.003 copying errors per genome each time it divides. ([BioNumbers](https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=106788))
- Living matter holds carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus at about 106:16:1. ([Redfield ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redfield_ratio))
- About a tenth of what one level of a food chain makes reaches the next. ([Ecological efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_efficiency))
- Among bacteria, bigger cells live faster for their size; among complex single cells, metabolism rises in step with size; in animals it rises with size to the three-quarters power. ([DeLong 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2919978))

### The ladder, with what triggered each step

1. ~4.2 Ga. Living on hydrogen and carbon dioxide at vents. Needs no oxygen.
2. By ~3.5 Ga. Methane-making. Same inputs.
3. Fermenters. Need the dead matter the first cells left.
4. By 3.4 Ga. Light without oxygen. Needs light plus sulfide or dissolved iron.
5. ~3.47 Ga. Sulfate-breathing. Makes the sulfide the light-users need.
6. ~3.4 Ga. Layered mats. Each layer uses its neighbour's waste.
7. By 3.2 Ga. Nitrogen fixing. Usable nitrogen was scarce.
8. 3.4–2.5 Ga. Light with oxygen. Needs only light and water.
9. 3.2–2.8 Ga. Oxygen-breathing, wherever oxygen collected.
10. 2.46–2.06 Ga. Oxygen fills the air. The old life retreats; defences are needed.
11. ~2.5–2.3 Ga. Nitrate-breathing. Needs nitrate, which needs oxygen.
12. ~2.05–1.2 Ga. The mitochondrion: one cell living inside another.
13. By ~1.8–1.65 Ga. Nucleus, swallowing prey, sex.
14. ~1.9–1.3 Ga. Algae: a complex cell keeps a light-harvester.
15. 1.63–1.05 Ga. Simple bodies, under pressure from predators that swallow small prey.
16. 575–539 Ma. Large animals, once oxygen allowed.
17. From 538.8 Ma. Predators, eyes, armour.
18. ~470–375 Ma. Land.

## Part 2. How the young planet worked

### Timeline

| When | What |
| --- | --- |
| 4.4 Ga | Liquid water and crust already, from a single ancient crystal ([Wilde 2001](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v409y2001i6817d10.1038_35051550.html)) |
| 4.2–3.4 Ga | Heavy bombardment, now thought prolonged and not one late spike (contested) ([Zellner 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06694)) |
| 2.5 Ga | Land above the sea probably 2–3% of the surface, against 27% now ([Charnay 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06265)) |
| 2.43–2.22 Ga | Oxygen first appears in the air, comes and goes for 200 million years, then stays ([Poulton 2021](https://www.leeds.ac.uk/news/article/4797/)) |
| 2.45–2.22 Ga | Three ice ages, at least one reaching the tropics ([Kopp 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1183582/)) |
| 1.8–0.8 Ga | A billion years in which little changed |
| 717–635 Ma | Two more global ice ages ([Rooney 2015](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1473458)) |
| 850–540 Ma | Oxygen rises toward today's level |

### The air

- Before oxygen: nitrogen much as now; carbon dioxide 10 to 2,500 times today's; methane 100 to 10,000 times; hydrogen at tens of parts in a million; oxygen under a millionth of today's. ([Catling & Zahnle 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7043912))
- When methane passes about a tenth of the carbon dioxide, an orange haze forms and cools the planet by up to 20 degrees.
- An ozone shield needs oxygen at 1% of today's by older models and 5–10% by newer ones (contested). ([Cooke 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11675))

### The Sun and the thermostat

- The Sun was 26% dimmer at 4.0 Ga, 21% at 3.0, 15% at 2.0 and 8% at 1.0. ([Feulner 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1204.4449))
- Carbon dioxide and methane kept the seas liquid. A thousandth of an atmosphere of methane adds about 14 degrees.
- Rock weathers faster when it is warm and the air holds more carbon dioxide, and weathering locks carbon dioxide away as limestone. Volcanoes return it. Over about a million years the two balance, which held the early Earth between roughly 0 and 50 °C. ([Krissansen-Totton 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5910859))
- When oxygen destroyed the methane, about 10 degrees of warming went with it, and the ice came.

### The sea

- Slightly acid at first (pH about 6.6), neutral by 2.5 Ga, 8.2 today.
- Full of dissolved iron and almost empty of sulfate (under 2.5 millionths of a mole per litre, against 28 thousandths today). ([Crowe 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25378621))
- Close to saturated with silica, because nothing alive took it out. ([Conley 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2017.00397/full))
- Wherever iron-rich deep water met oxygen or iron-using light-harvesters, the iron fell out as rust. Those layers, laid down from 3.8 to 1.85 Ga, are the world's iron ore.
- Phosphate at 10–25% of today's, because there was little land to weather and iron rust carries phosphate down with it. ([Bjerrum & Canfield 2002](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v417y2002i6885d10.1038_417159a.html))

### Ultraviolet

- With no ozone, damage to DNA in surface water was about 1,000 times today's. Thirty metres down it matched today's sea surface. ([Cockell 2000](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/en/publications/ultraviolet-radiation-and-the-photobiology-of-earths-early-oceans))
- Life near the top survived under mineral grains, in mats, and by moving down.

### Food and energy for the first life

- Hot acid vents: over 400 °C, rich in sulfide and iron. Cooler alkaline vents: 40–91 °C, pH 9–11, rich in hydrogen and methane.
- Lightning fixed nitrogen, perhaps a hundred times less once carbon dioxide fell (contested).
- Before any cell split water for its hydrogen, all life was limited by what the rock supplied: hydrogen first, then dissolved iron, then sulfur. The living world made at most a fortieth of what today's sea makes. ([Kharecha 2005](https://vpl.uw.edu/publication/a-coupled-atmosphere-ecosystem-model-of-the-early-archean-earth-geobiology-2005-2), [Ward 2019](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/5p2e2-mmn05))

### Moon and tides

- At 3.2 Ga the Moon was at 70% of its present distance and a day lasted about 13 hours. ([Eulenfeld & Heubeck](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05464))
- Tides at 3.9 Ga were about 3.4 times today's, with a high tide every 6.8 hours. ([Davies 2021](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-1582.html))

### The layered sea

- A tenth of what the lit surface makes sinks out of it, and a hundredth reaches the bed. That strips the surface of nutrients; life at the top depends on deep water being brought back up. ([Sarmiento 2004](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v427y2004i6969d10.1038_nature02127.html))

### Cause and effect, for the engine

1. A brighter star warms the planet; warmth speeds weathering; weathering takes carbon dioxide out; the temperature holds.
2. Oxygen rises; methane is destroyed; the greenhouse fails; ice.
3. No oxygen, no ozone: life stays deep or under cover.
4. Deep water without oxygen fills with dissolved iron; where it wells up into light or oxygen it rusts out.
5. Nothing uses silica, so the sea is full of it.
6. Until a cell splits water, life is capped by the rock's supply of hydrogen and iron.
7. Little land, little phosphorus; more land, more.
8. Sinking remains starve the surface unless deep water returns.
9. A closer Moon means shorter days and bigger, more frequent tides.

## Part 3. Where the engine stands against this, and the build order

### What already matches

- Matter and energy are conserved: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, salt, silicon and water all have books that balance.
- Free oxygen starts at nothing, is made only by light-harvesters, and is soaked up by dissolved iron first.
- Genes pass sideways between touching cells. New genes arise by copying errors.
- A hunter can swallow a light-harvester and keep it: the step that made algae.
- Sex, bodies, cell fates, walls, shells, holdfasts, predators and prey all exist as ordinary genes.
- The sea has depth: light fades, vents feed only the bed, cells swim and are stirred in three dimensions.

### What does not

| On Earth | In the engine now |
| --- | --- |
| The first cells lived on hydrogen and carbon dioxide at vents, in the dark | The first cell harvests light and gives off oxygen from its first moment |
| Hydrogen, sulfide and methane were real substances that cells made and used | Vent chemistry is one number per site; there is no hydrogen, sulfide or methane |
| Five ways of living came before oxygen: acetate-making, methane-making, fermenting, light without oxygen, sulfate-breathing | One light gene (always makes oxygen), one vent gene, one for dissolved food, one for remains |
| Breathing oxygen pays up to 15 times what fermenting does | It pays twice |
| Oxygen poisoned life that had no defence | Oxygen only helps |
| Only lightning fixed nitrogen until cells learned to, and oxygen destroys the enzyme | No cell fixes nitrogen |
| A swallower without mitochondria cannot make a living | Every eater starves: the engine's oldest unsolved problem, and probably the same one |
| The young Sun was a quarter to a third dimmer; greenhouse gases and rock weathering held the temperature | Temperature is a setting. Nothing ties it to the star or the air |
| No ozone until oxygen: the surface and the land were bathed in ultraviolet | No ultraviolet |
| The Moon was close and tides were large | No tides |
| Surface water and deep water hold different things; sinking remains strip the surface of nutrients | Each column of sea is one well-mixed stock |

Since this table was written, four rows have been closed: hydrogen is a real substance, breathing pays fifteen times, a cell can fix nitrogen, and a swallower needs kept breathers to make a living. See Progress below.

### Build order

Each stage ends with a measurement on several planets, not one.

**Stage 1. The chemistry of a world without oxygen.**
Hydrogen and sulfide come out of vents as real dissolved substances. Methane is a gas in the air. The vent gene becomes what it was on Earth: hydrogen plus carbon dioxide into living matter. New genes: methane-making, light without oxygen (it uses sulfide or dissolved iron and leaves sulfur or rust), sulfate-breathing, fermenting what others leave.
*Gate: a planet seeded only with the hydrogen-eater holds a living population at its vents with no light-harvester on it.*

**Stage 2. Oxygen as revolution and as poison.**
Without oxygen a cell gets about a fifteenth of the energy in its food. Breathing oxygen becomes a gene, as does a defence against it. Oxygen injures cells that have no defence. Nitrogen-fixing becomes a gene that oxygen switches off.
*Gate: when oxygen rises, undefended kinds fall back to the dark bed and defended breathers take the surface; eaters that breathe hold their own without restocking.*

**Stage 3. An Earth-like beginning.**
A way to start a world as Earth started: no oxygen, one kind of cell at the vents, everything else left to arise. The ladder of life's steps follows Earth's order.
*Gate: on some planets, light-harvesting and then oxygen arise unaided from the vent cell, and how long it takes is reported.*

**Stage 4. The young planet.**
Ultraviolet that water and, later, ozone shut out. A star that brightens with age, a greenhouse of carbon dioxide and methane, and rock weathering that draws carbon dioxide down faster when it is warm and delivers phosphorus and silica to the sea. Tides that wet and dry the shores.
*Gate: a planet whose oxygen rises loses its methane and cools; life is absent from the surface until ozone forms.*

**Stage 5. A layered sea.**
Surface and deep water keep separate stocks. Sinking remains carry nutrients down; upwelling brings them back.
*Gate: surface water runs short of nutrients away from upwelling, as it does on Earth.*

**Stage 6. Complex cells, bodies, animals, land.**
A swallowed oxygen-breather kept alive inside its host, as the mitochondrion was. Then the existing bodies, predators and land rules, retuned against the new chemistry.

## Progress

### Stage 1, first step: vents and hydrogen (2026-10-11)

Built:

- **Standing vent fields.** A planet is made with narrow lines of vents across its sea bed (about 250 of 3,200 sites on a small planet), each with a strength from nothing to one. Before this a planet had no vents at all until a random eruption made a hot spot.
- **Hydrogen as a real dissolved substance.** Vents pour it into the water over them; a tenth of what is dissolved leaks away each cycle. After 40 cycles the water over vents held 28 times what the open sea did.
- **The vent gene lives on it.** Hydrogen and carbon dioxide are joined into living matter, at a rate that matches a light-feeder under a bright star, and only on or near the bed. The planet's vents together supply far less than its star, so what limits vent life is the rock's supply, as it was on Earth.
- **The Archaeon starter** is set down at vents, born with its catalyst, and keeps the membrane transporter that sets how fast a cell can burn fuel.

Measured against the gate (*a planet seeded only with the hydrogen-eater holds a living population at its vents with no light-harvester on it*), on three planets with no restocking:

| Cycles | Cells |
| --- | --- |
| 300 | 999 to 1,156 |
| 600 | 200 to 238 |
| 900 | 84 and 89 (two planets run on) |
| 1,200 | 56 and 57 |

At 1,200 cycles 55 of 56 cells were on or beside a vent and 51 were gripping the bed. The early thousand lived on the planet's starting stock of dissolved food; the population then fell toward what the vents supply. It was still falling slowly at 1,200 cycles, so the gate is met only provisionally: the level it settles at has not been seen.

Not yet built in Stage 1: sulfide, methane, light without oxygen, sulfate-breathing, fermenting as its own gene.

### Stage 2, and the first animals (2026-10-11)

Built:

- **Breathing pays fifteen times what fermenting does.** A unit of food holds 22.5 units of energy. A cell that breathes it with oxygen captures all of it; one that cannot captures a fifteenth. Life without oxygen runs exactly as it did before; life with oxygen is 7.5 times better off.
- **Breathing is a gene.** Its machinery sits in the cell's outer membrane. A mouth takes most of that membrane, so a cell that swallows prey breathes at about a sixth of full rate, and a cell four times the usual size has too little membrane for its bulk and breathes at 60%.
- **Complex cells.** A mouth that swallows a breather now and then keeps it alive inside, as the mitochondrion was kept. From then on it breathes fully whatever its membrane is given over to, and its descendants inherit the breathers. Every starter that is a complex cell on Earth begins with them; the bacteria and the archaeon do not.
- **Nitrogen fixing is a gene.** It takes nitrogen from the air at 80 units of energy a unit. Oxygen stops it unless the cell is walled or deep inside a body, and a cell that is making oxygen from light cannot fix beside it. A new starter, Anabaena, is a walled thread that harvests light by day and fixes in the dark.
- **Nitrogen sets how much life a planet holds.** With energy cheap, producers filled a planet to its cell limit within 200 cycles and nothing else could breed. The sea's starting nitrogen was cut to a third, so nitrogen runs out first, as it does in the open ocean.
- **Keeping a light-feeder makes an alga, not a better hunter.** A hunter that kept a swallowed light-feeder used to harvest light at full rate and hunt at full rate, and kept one within a few hundred cycles; every eater ended up living 70 to 85% on light and nothing ever died. Now a kept light-feeder costs the mouth, as a cell's own light machinery always did, keeping one is twenty times rarer, and the pair can take up dissolved nitrogen as an alga does.
- **Two animals with a life cycle.** Jelly is a body of cells that stay together, swim after the scent of life, sting what they touch and swallow it. Hydra is the same fixed to the bed. Each stops growing at the sixth division and sheds single cells that begin the next body.
- A swimmer could climb out of the sea into the sky on a flat world. Thrust now levels off at the surface.

Measured:

| Test | Result |
| --- | --- |

| Share of fuel breathed: no machinery, its own, with a mouth, a mouth and kept breathers | 0%, 95%, 17%, 98% |

Against the gate (*eaters that breathe hold their own without restocking*): met in a pond with plenty of prey, where the animal multiplies tenfold. On a whole planet over 1,200 cycles a few Jelly bodies persist unaided but do not grow in number, and Hydra dies out. In a sea crowded with every starter, eaters hold near the restocking floor.

What limits them now is known. Before today an eater took 0.012 to 0.022 units of food a cycle and needed 0.035 to 0.05. It now burns 0.007 to 0.017, so it no longer starves, but its surplus is small and a new body takes hundreds of cycles. A mouth is feeding about one moment in twenty: the rest of the time it has found nothing it can swallow. Doubling the reach of the feeding current did not raise what eaters caught, so that change was not kept.

Not yet built in Stages 1 and 2: sulfide, methane, light without oxygen, sulfate-breathing, fermenting as its own gene, oxygen as a poison and a defence against it. Oxygen as a poison waits for the layered sea (Stage 5): with one stock of oxygen per column there would be nowhere for life without the defence to go.

Not re-measured since these changes: the vent-only planets of the first step. Nothing in their chemistry changed, since they have no oxygen, but they now start with a third of the nitrogen.

### The sky, the air's layers, and animals again (2026-10-12)

**Why the animals starved.** Four causes, each found by measurement on the three planets of the last section.

| Cause | Measured | Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wounds never healed | Mean injury 0.2–0.6 in every species; deaths by injury outnumbered starvation four to one. Only cells with the repair gene mended, and daughters inherited wounds. | Every cell mends, slowly. Mean injury fell to 0.1–0.2. |
| A body fed no better than one cell | Twenty mouths drew from the same 1.5 units as one, for twenty times the upkeep. | A body's mouths pump together; the inflow falls with the square of distance, so reach and clearance grow with the number of mouths. |
| A grown body never bred again | Jelly cells were 4–7 generations old after 1,200 cycles: division stopped at the sixth and only a few tip cells ever left. | Cells divide for as long as they are fed; a cell with one neighbour leaves as a bud from the fourth division on. In a pond, 8 Jelly cells became 187 in 400 cycles (83 before), with 15 bodies at once. |
| Hunters became algae | Half of all Hunters and Paramecium, and a third of Jelly, carried a kept light-feeder and had stopped eating. | Keeping one is 25 times rarer. After it, 0–10% carried one, with one planet at 34–39%. |

A cell in a Jelly body caught 0.0035 fuel a cycle and burned 0.0074; a loose Paramecium caught 0.0085 and burned 0.014. Motors were a twentieth of the burn. Five body plans were released together on three planets: the continuous-budding clump, the old cycle, a thread, a big-celled form with no sting, and one that buds only when fed. No plan won everywhere: each died out on one or two planets and reached 40–210 cells on the others. The clump was largest where it lived (210 cells, one body of 158) and was kept. A Hydra built as a stalk was the only Hydra to survive anywhere, and was kept.

**The sky.** Caden asked for asteroids that bring resources and chaos until a good atmosphere blocks most of them, and then that the atmosphere be modelled by what each of its layers does. What the layers do, from the sources below: weather and nearly all the water vapour are in the troposphere; ozone in the stratosphere absorbs ultraviolet; most meteors burn up in the mesosphere; the thermosphere is the first to meet the star's most energetic radiation; the exosphere thins into space. An ozone shield needs free oxygen, between one and ten hundredths of today's by different estimates. The engine now has each of these except the troposphere's warming (see `docs/CREATOR_POWERS.md`).

Two things about the request do not match Earth and were not built as asked. Falling rock is stopped by the air's mass, in the thin air high up, so it is the air that shields; but only small bodies, at any thickness. And the heavy bombardment ended because the planet swept up the loose rock near it, not because the air thickened. Both are in: the rate falls with age, and thicker air stops more of what still comes.

### Muscle (2026-10-12)

Caden's goal that day: animals "moving around using muscles, and eating/fighting with complex systems", across the periods of the geologic timeline (see `docs/GEOLOGIC_TIMELINE.md`).

**Why nothing ever moved by muscle.** A row of twelve cells with muscle, nerve and bristles travelled 0.017 units a cycle. The same row with a coat of cilia travelled 0.69. Four causes, found by tracing one beat tick by tick:

| Cause | Measured | Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cells were hard spheres already touching | A muscle set to shorten 22% shortened the body 2% | A working muscle flattens the two cells it joins against each other as it shortens |
| The signal that fires the nerve also held the muscle half tight | Tightness swung between 0.50 and 0.86 | A muscle with a nerve twitches when the nerve fires and lets go between. A twitch is all or nothing |
| Muscle pulled about as hard as one cell's cilia push | The ends of the body moved and the middle did not | A working muscle pulls far harder than an ordinary bond holds (real muscle outpulls cilia thousands of times for its cross-section) |
| A nerve fired once in 8 cycles | 5 beats in 40 cycles | Nerves are eight times quicker: a beat takes a little over a cycle |

Muscle work was also charged at twelve times the price of the same work done by cilia; it is now priced the same.

| Row of twelve cells, 40 cycles | Distance | Energy spent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cilia | 27.5 units | 100.6 |
| Muscle, before | 0.7 units | 0.3 |
| Muscle, now | 6.5 units in 35 beats | 17.3 |

So muscle now carries a body a quarter as fast as full-strength cilia for a sixth of the energy: cheaper for the distance. Ciliated bodies on a planet do not run their cilia at full strength; Jelly bodies there move at 0.07–0.17 units a cycle, and a Worm's body at about 0.11.

**The Worm.** A starter animal built from ordinary genes. Its larva is one cell with a mouth, a sting, weak cilia, an eye-spot and a rudder. Three genes are switched on by having neighbours: muscle, the nerve that fires it, and the signal that keeps the nerve firing. A fourth, bristles, needs a neighbour on each side. The cilia gene is switched off the same way. So a larva swims with cilia and a grown body only by muscle. It buds as Jelly does.

A first design with fifteen extra genes, mouths only at the two ends and food passed down the body died out on six planets of six: a body of sixteen cells with two mouths caught 0.003–0.005 fuel a cell a cycle and burned 0.011. The lean design above has a mouth on every cell.

In a pond of prey, 8 larvae became 41 cells in 400 cycles, with bodies of up to 14 cells, 47 muscles pulling at once, the cilia put away, moving at up to 0.2 units a cycle, and 106 units of prey taken.

**A rule built and taken out again.** The first thing built from this was "filter or jaw": a mouth with mineral in it drew no water and got little of a loose cell lighter than itself, so that animals with bone in the head would have to eat other animals. Asked whether a run should check that "jaws make carnivores", Caden answered: "what do you mean do jaws make carnivores? A simple google search will tell you that jaws are just part of eating." He is right. The paper the rule leaned on says filter feeders take smaller prey than raptorial ones; it does not say a hard mouth cannot filter. The rule is gone. In the engine a jaw is what it was before: mineral in a mouth, which helps against armour and a body's grip.

**A third round: why any predator passes up small, easy food.** This is the question under the missing carnivores, and it has a classical answer.

- *A predator cannot search while it is handling what it has caught.* Holling's disc equation: intake rises with prey density and then levels off at one item per handling time, however much prey there is.
- *So an item is worth its energy divided by the time it takes to handle,* and a predator does best by ignoring the poor items whenever the good ones are common enough. Bluegill sunfish given water fleas of three sizes ate them as they met them when prey was scarce, and dropped the smaller sizes one after another as it became plentiful.
- *Fish that eat plankton hunt by eye and take the largest first,* because the largest are the easiest to see (Brooks and Dodson 1965).

The engine has none of this. A mouth takes from every cell it touches, all at once, every tick, with no time spent on any of them. Under that rule eating everything is always the best policy, and no eater can ever gain by specialising. A comment in the code says "Engulfing is fast and takes one cell at a time", but nothing enforces it.

What it suggests: one prey to a mouth at a time, held for a handling time (the bite that holds on is half of this already); and the smallest prey a mouth bothers with as something its genes can set. Then a plankton cell costs a mouth the same time as an animal's cell and gives a fraction of the tissue, and where larger prey is common a mouth that waits for it wins. This is the first proposal here that makes a carnivore something evolution could find, where the others only made room for one.

**The first half is built.** A mouth now takes one thing at a time. While it is biting a cell it bites no other, and after its last bite it is taken up for one unit of time (four ticks) before it can start on the next. At that length, by arithmetic from the pond figures and not by measurement, a mouth filling itself on the smallest cells can take two or three times what its body needs and no more, so nothing that lives on plankton should be starved by the rule, while a cell of 0.4 is worth four or five plankton cells of a mouth's time. Digestion by enzymes on contact, which is how grazers and the Trilobite feed, is not a mouth and is unchanged. The second half, a gene for the smallest prey a mouth accepts, is not built: it is a 54th gene, with the saved-world handling that goes with one. Not measured on a planet. The unit tests, which include animals growing by what they catch, pass.

**A fourth round: why warm-blooded land animals die out here.** Every period from the Triassic on is tested by animals that make their own heat, and in every run the Dinosaur, Bird, Mammal and Ape starve.

*The fault.* A cell's pace is set by its site: warmth times how wet the ground is, and dry ground alone cuts pace to 0.45. A warm-blooded cell made up the whole shortfall, so on dry ground it was paying for heat to make up for *dryness*: 0.15 a cycle, against 0.055 for everything else a cell pays and 0.025 for the same animal's heat in the sea. *Changed.* Heat now makes up for cold and nothing else. A warm-blooded cell on dry ground is slowed by the dryness as any cell is and pays only for the tenth that the cold takes. For each unit of work done it now costs about 1.5 times a cold-blooded cell on the same ground, where it cost 3.7 times; near the poles, where the cold takes more, about three times for 40% more pace. Also not built: heat lost faster to water than to air.

**A mouth is as big as the cells that make it.** Caden: "how are we doing mouth? Shouldn't mouths be a bunch of cells that let an organism swallow other organisms. Mouths may be of different sizes allowing them to eat bigger prey."

*How it was.* A mouth was one cell. Any cell with the mouth gene bit what it touched, and what it could take was set by the bulk of its *whole body* against one cell of the prey. So one small mouth cell on a large body could take nearly anything, the size of a mouth meant nothing, and nothing was ever swallowed whole. The Inspect panel even said so: "The whole body's bulk decides what it can swallow."

*What the record says.* Fish that eat fish swallow their prey whole, and the largest prey they take is set by how wide the mouth opens against how deep the prey's body is; largemouth bass in one study never took prey wider than their mouths. The sizes a predator cannot get down are a refuge, and those refuges shape which sizes and kinds of prey a water holds. (One review warns that "gape" is measured a dozen different ways.) The first animals with a true mouth were the two-layered ones, the jellyfish kind: an opening into a sac of cells that digest.

*Changed.* A mouth is now the mouth cells of a body that hold on to one another, and its size is their bulk together.

- What a mouth can take is set by the mouth: nothing more than a quarter larger than itself, at full speed what is two thirds its size or less. One mouth cell on a body of eight plain cells bites exactly as a mouth cell alone does.
- A body whose whole bulk fits the mouth goes in whole. Its hold on its own cells, which is what makes a body a refuge from small mouths, is no help against a mouth bigger than all of it.
- The feeding current draws in only what the body's largest mouth could swallow.
- A single-celled hunter is its own mouth, so nothing changes for it. A sponge, which is all mouth, is unchanged too.

The planned animals already grow mouths of many cells at the head: 7 of the Worm's 16 cells, 9 of the Sea scorpion's 28, 21 of the Fish's 36. The Shrimp and Copepod carry theirs along their limbs, a few small cells to each, so they can take plankton and nothing much larger, while a Fish's mouth is bigger than a whole Copepod. That is a food chain set by mouth size, on paper. Not measured on a planet; 181 unit tests pass, one of them new.

*Not built.* A gut: prey is still eaten at the mouth, and is not carried inside a body to be digested there. What a mouth holds moves with it, so its weight drags on the eater in air and hardly at all in water, where a body weighs next to nothing; but it is not added to the load that can crush an animal's legs on land. And each animal's mouth is not yet sized to its diet: the Fish is more than half mouth, which no fish is.

**A fifth round: the grown animals were being eaten alive, one cell at a time.** Caden said "run it", and one planet four times the size of the earlier ones (size 256, limit 64,000 cells, seed 5) was run for 1,800 cycles with the cast released in Earth's order. The record of that run is in `docs/GEOLOGIC_TIMELINE.md`. Every planned animal but the Trilobite died out: Worm, Shrimp, Copepod, Sea scorpion, Fish, Amphibian, Dragonfly, Reptile, Dinosaur, Bird and Ape to nothing, Mammal to 5 cells.

*What they died of.* The engine now keeps a ledger: for every starter species, how many cells starved, wasted, or died of each kind of injury, and whose mouths the living tissue went into (`Habitat::deaths`, `Habitat::taken`; the batch run prints both). They were not starving: energy for their size was as good as anything's on the planet. Single cells were taking them apart. A body cell of these animals is small (0.2 of tissue) and a Hunter or a Paramecium is one large cell (0.7), so by the mouth rule of the round before, each of them had a mouth three times the size of any one cell of a fish. The body's hold on its cells was divided by exactly that ratio, so the bigger the eater's cell beside the prey's, the less being in a body helped. A bacterium was eating a reptile.

*What the record says.*

- A colony is a refuge from whatever cannot swallow it whole. A single-celled alga kept for thousands of generations as single cells, given a flagellate that eats by engulfing, became colonies within a hundred generations, settling at eight cells, and those were "virtually immune" to it (Boraas, Seale and Boxhorn 1998). A later study found groups protect against a small engulfer and not against a large filter-feeding animal: the refuge depends on the eater's size beside the whole group, not beside one cell of it.
- There are single cells that damage tissue they cannot swallow: the amoeba of dysentery bites pieces off cells. That is disease, and animals meet it with skin, mucus and immunity.
- A mouth of many cells is opened by muscle. A Hydra's mouth is a sealed sheet of cells that is pulled open by rings and spokes of contractile fibre, and a muscle relaxant stops it opening (Carter and others 2016). A sponge has no mouth and no gut: each of its cells takes in particles smaller than itself and digests them inside.

*Changed.*

- **A cell in a body is held by the body, however big the mouth.** A mouth that cannot take the whole body has to tear the cell out, and what it pulls against is every tie that cell has to its body and the matrix laid down around it. With three ties and no matrix a cell is taken at a tenth the rate of a loose one; with matrix, at less. Being bigger than the one cell no longer helps. Teeth still cut through, as before, so tearing flesh from a body larger than the mouth now takes teeth.
- **Matrix slows enzymes working in from outside**, by up to nine times for a cell fully bound in it. The limbs' ends of the planned animals have none, as before, since they are the young that come away.
- **A mouth of many cells needs muscle in it to open as one.** Without muscle each mouth cell takes what it can alone. The line above that said a sponge was unchanged by the mouth rule was wrong: a sponge is all mouth cells joined together, so it had become one mouth the size of its body, and it was what ate the Fish. It is now many small mouths again. So are the Jelly and the Hydra here, which have no muscle gene.

**A sixth round: a wide mouth cannot keep hold of the smallest things.** This is the other half of the missing carnivores. Nothing here chose animal prey, because nothing had a reason to pass up plankton.

*What the record says.* In fish that take in water with their food, what is kept is set by the spacing of the rakers across the gills: prey smaller than the gaps goes out with the water. Fish that live on plankton have many long, fine, close-set rakers; fish that eat fish have few, short and wide apart, and the gaps widen as the fish grows, so the smallest thing it can eat grows with it. It is a matter of what can be held, not a preference.

*Built.* A mouth of many cells, opened by muscle, keeps nothing smaller than a sixteenth of its own bulk (`LEAK`, chosen and not measured). One cell that wraps itself round its food has no such limit, so the small things are for small mouths. A body small enough to go in whole is judged by the whole of it, and a piece torn from a larger body is held by the bite. The feeding current no longer draws in what every mouth of the body would lose.

*Measured, and it changed nothing that could be seen.* The same fifty cycles from cycle 900 of the big planet, read by the new record of who ate whom. With the animals set down in the same places, a Fish's food was 30% sponge, 29% of the smallest bacteria and 25% coral with the rule, and the same within two points in a run where the rule did not apply to it. The reason is in the Fish's body: its main mouth is 14 cells and now keeps nothing under 0.16 of tissue, but it also has two or three small patches of mouth cells and the mouths at the ends of its limbs, and those go on taking bacteria. Every planned animal is built that way, because the ends of the limbs are its young, and the young are single cells that have to eat.

*Two mistakes on the way, both mine.* The rule was first written with bristles as a strainer that lifts the limit, and to make that fit, fins were taken off the planned animals' heads. In that run the Fish ate 67% grazers and 26% sponge, and that was reported to Caden as a first real carnivore. It was not: taking the fins off changed the bodies, so different numbers of animals were set down in different places, and three fish that land among grazers eat grazers. It also broke the Worm, which lost the bristles on its front half, and the Trilobite's young (two unit tests failed). The fins are back, bristles are not a strainer, and the claim is withdrawn. A comparison of what a few animals eat over fifty cycles is only worth anything when they are set down in the same places.

*So the missing carnivores are still missing.* What this round did find is where the small food goes in: not through the big mouth. An adult that is to live on large prey must have no small mouths, which means its young cannot be the ends of its own limbs. That is eggs as a stage of their own, which is on the not-built list.

**A seventh round: why plants stay at the water's edge.** Caden, 2026-10-12: "I think you were going to add proper mating pollination etc for trees and grass etc. and have the spores travel by air, maybe that would help grow more on the land?"

*What the planet showed.* On the second big planet at cycle 1,800 there were about 1,600 cells of walled plant out of the water, half of them within 0.02 units of the waterline and nineteen in twenty within one unit. A fifth of the planet (2,796 of 12,800 sites) was land holding a trace of water or none, with almost no dead matter on it (median nothing). Each plant had 44 to 91 single cells of its own kind about, and two in three of those or more were already gripping the ground where they had been shed. Almost none lay loose on dry ground. Pollination was not what was missing: the land had no water in it, and a loose plant cell gripped wherever it came down.

*Why the land was dry.* Rain fell on it as on the sea, but bare ground gave it back to the air at the same rate as open water and let the rest run off.

*What the record says.* Seeds and pollen freed plants from needing a film of water to breed in, which mosses and ferns still need because their sperm swim. Plants here have never needed water to breed, so there is no obstacle of that kind to remove. Living away from wet ground took soil, and the first soils were made by the plants and fungi themselves: Early Devonian soils in Yunnan are bound by networks of rhizomes, a thousand to the square metre, and the oldest plants in the Rhynie chert already have fungi living in them. In modern ground, litter cuts what the soil loses to the air (by 13 and 27% under 2 and 5 cm of pine litter in one study) and dead matter holds water.

*Built.*

- **Soil.** Remains lying at a site hold water there: five times their own amount, up to 0.02 of depth, twice what keeps a cell wet (`SPONGE`, `SOAK`). Held water does not run off and dries at a quarter the rate of open water (`SHELTER`). All three numbers are chosen, not measured. No water is made or lost: the planet's water still balances.
- **Spores on the wind.** A loose cell falling through the air on a planet is carried sideways four times as fast as one blown along the ground (`LOFT`), and falls no slower for it. Before, the wind moved only a loose cell already lying on dry ground.

Soil works: the plants at the edge stopped dying of thirst and all five kinds grew where three had been shrinking. One planet run once each way, so the direction is clear and the size is not.

*A wrong measure, and what the right one says.* "Above the waterline" was the wrong way to ask whether plants had left the edge: the ground near a shore is low, so a plant several sites from the water is still only a fraction of a unit above it. The count is now by ground: `inland` is plant cells standing on a site with no open water on it (only what its soil holds), and `interior` is those three or more sites from the nearest open water. By that count the plants were already off the wet edge before soil: 923 of the 1,591 cells at the end of the second big planet stood on ground with no open water, 131 of them in the interior. On the first big planet, where single cells still ate the plants, none were in the interior.

Without soil the interior was emptying (131 cells to 73); with it, it grew (131 to 170). So Caden's guess was right in direction: more grows on the land. Which of the two rules did it is not separated, and it is one planet run once each way. An earlier line in this section, sent to Caden as "inland spread does not work yet", was wrong and is withdrawn.

*Tried and taken out: a spore fate.* The land plants were given the bud life cycle the Jelly and Hydra have, with the holdfast as well as the glue switched off in the bud's fate, so that a cell at the end of a plant let go of plant and ground together. Two hundred cycles after 31 such plants were set down among 1,700 of the old kind, they had made spores (80 cells in the spore fate, 183 bodies from 31) and the spores had gripped again within a tick or two of coming loose: 100 of their 119 single cells were anchored and none lay loose. A cell alone is an ordinary cell again at once, and a cell that carries a little water of its own passes the test for damp ground wherever it lands. No gain could be seen over the old plants beside them, so the body plan is as it was.

*Not built.* A spore that stays a spore until it has travelled: that needs a cell alone to be able to keep a fate, which no cell here can. Sex for the Tree and the Grass (the Flower has it) would add variation and no ground; it would also make the Cretaceous test, which asks for plants that breed by fusing cells, pass in the Carboniferous, so that test would need a new meaning first.
